I agree with the long-sustain conclusions and most of the immediate experiment order. I do not agree with several of §19’s interpretations, or with making further distillation conditional on finding a simple rule in the search ablation.

The new measurements are valuable. But §19 moves too quickly from “this optimization run barely changed the student” to “argmax imitation has almost no headroom” and “single-forward CE cannot transfer continuation valuation.” Those are not established.
1. The long-sustain experiment gave a clear, useful answer

The λ20 rejection is justified. You now have mutually supporting evidence:

    Increasing inventory and stack height.
    Late deaths on six of ten tapes.
    Unhealthy final states even among survivors.
    Little improvement in Tetris rate over λ10.

λ10 is the better candidate on this panel, with continuing Tetrises and apparently bounded inventory. Calling it viable but unpromoted is appropriate.

One qualification: the pooled occupancy windows are encouraging, but inspect per-game trajectories too. With ten games, a stable average can obscure individual upward trends. This does not undermine the result; it is simply worth checking before promotion.

The factorial potential × completion-bonus experiment remains reasonable. It can identify whether both ingredients are necessary and whether completion reward helps release inventory rather than merely increasing clears.
2. Rare death-rejection decisions could explain a large survival gain

I disagree strongly with:

    Death-rejection ablation expectation is now LOW (0.33% of states).

For survival, 0.33% of decisions is not necessarily rare enough to be unimportant.

As a deliberately simplified illustration, an independent lethal error probability of 0.0033 per placement corresponds to an expected waiting time of roughly 300 placements. Even 0.001 per placement is consequential over a 1500-piece game.

Your observed fraction is not such a hazard estimate: the states are correlated, come from incumbent trajectories, and the events concentrate near death. But that is precisely why the fraction alone cannot dismiss death rejection.

There are two competing explanations:

    Late symptom: the policy was already doomed; rejecting the root only postpones death.
    Recoverable trap: the policy could recover, but chooses a placement that makes the preview impossible.

The proposed ablation directly distinguishes their practical importance. I would keep it at high priority, not treat it as an expected negative.

Also record:

    How often the filter changes an action.
    Survival after each intervention.
    Whether interventions restore a lower-risk board or merely delay death.
    Cases where every root is dead.

The full-game effect matters far more than the bank frequency.
3. “No learning at this budget” is supported; “no headroom” is not

The fit probe establishes:

    At lr 1e-5, after 400 batch-64 updates, this training setup produced essentially no improvement in measured teacher agreement or regret.

That is useful—but quite narrow.

There are 17,430 training states and 25,600 sampled state presentations. If sampling is approximately uniform, this is only about 1.5 passes through the training states. With a conservative learning rate, no material movement does not distinguish:

    Too little optimization.
    Poor loss scaling.
    Gradients dominated by the wrong states.
    An implementation issue.
    An input limitation.
    A genuine capacity limitation.

Furthermore, roughly 21% disagreement leaves substantial behavioral headroom. Whether those disagreements matter is a separate question.

The teacher already produces a large closed-loop survival gain. If imitation accurately reproduced its choices on the states encountered during play, it would reproduce that policy’s benefit. There is no general obstacle whereby CE can learn an argmax but cannot learn it when the underlying reason is continuation valuation.

The open questions are whether the current student can represent those choices, whether the loss trains it effectively, and whether the data cover the necessary states.
Small value gaps do not establish low practical importance

These scores are not calibrated survival probabilities. A four-unit teacher preference can separate:

    Two equivalent placements.
    A recoverable and an unrecoverable setup.
    Two plans whose consequences emerge many placements later.

Likewise, median live regret of zero is expected when agreement is already around 79%. It does not characterize the disagreed-on decisions.

I would report separately:

    Agreement across all states.
    Agreement on baseline–teacher disagreements.
    Regret conditional on disagreement.
    Regret conditional on choosing a live root.
    Avoidable dead-root choices.
    All-roots-dead states.

For conventional readability, use positive regret:

[ \operatorname{regret}(s)=\max_a Q_T(s,a)-Q_T(s,a_{\text{student}}). ]

Do not average arbitrary −1e6 penalties together with ordinary ranking differences.

Also, the true-preview 1500-state diagnostic is not exactly the same scoring protocol as the full fast-path fit. Its live-regret result should not be treated as an exact decomposition of the original full-bank metric.
4. The fast-path piece-slot discrepancy needs resolution now

This is the most concerning new detail:

    Champion fast-path scoring uses cur for both piece slots; slow/fast differ by 0.1–0.6 units per candidate.

I would not classify this as harmless noise yet.

First, it conflicts with the earlier expectation that fast and slow scoring were numerically equivalent. Second, the document itself says important decisions involve 1-ply gaps of 0.3–5.5 units. A candidate-dependent perturbation of 0.1–0.6 can matter precisely there.

The comparison against a roughly 13-unit median margin is not sufficient. You need action changes and pairwise-gap changes, particularly on the disagreement states.

Separate two tasks:
Reproduce the incumbent

Keeping the fast-path behavior is correct when labeling the actual champion. Do not silently “fix” it and assume the champion is unchanged.
Establish the intended semantics

Document, for each scoring stage:

    What board the network receives.
    What each piece slot means.
    Which piece identity each path supplies.
    Which future pieces are legitimately known.

Then compare the incumbent with a separately named, semantically corrected variant.

Architectural access to board + current + preview does not establish input sufficiency if the actual scoring call duplicates a piece ID. Conversely, the teacher can still use preview through its expansion even when the leaf evaluator has that duplication.

This needs an explicit information-flow audit before declaring the student’s input check passed.
5. I would add a tiny-set overfit test before collecting more labels

The next fitting experiment need not be expensive or consume gameplay panels.

Take perhaps 128–512 states containing a balanced selection of:

    Teacher–baseline disagreements.
    Important live-root preferences.
    Avoidable preview deaths.
    Ordinary agreement states.

Then deliberately attempt to overfit them.

Use a small learning-rate/temperature sweep and compare CE with a pairwise preference loss. Track actual:

    Training loss.
    Teacher agreement.
    Teacher-preferred action margin.
    Gradient and parameter-update magnitudes.

This is a diagnostic, not a candidate for promotion. It is acceptable if the resulting weights play terribly.

The question is:

    Can this implementation make the network learn these specific decisions?

If it cannot, debug optimization, inputs, loss construction, and representation. If it can, expand to game-disjoint validation. If that succeeds but gameplay fails, proceed to student-state aggregation.

Also verify that −1e6 death sentinels are used for evaluation/masking, not accidentally treated as ordinary numeric regression targets or used inconsistently in the training objective.

Given the index-space bugs already found, I would add a synthetic test where a deliberately misranked, known teacher action becomes more likely after training. Teacher-label parity alone does not test the training update.
6. A simple search rule is not a prerequisite for distillation

I disagree with:

    Reformulated fit and aggregation only if item 3 shows a distillable rule.

That condition is backwards.

    If death rejection explains most of the gain: use the cheap rule first; neural distillation may be unnecessary.
    If continuation valuation explains the gain: that is a direct reason to investigate search distillation, not a reason to stop.
    If the student cannot fit even a small controlled dataset: then reconsider the student or training setup.

A teacher does not need to reduce to an obvious hand-coded rule to be learnable.
Recommended immediate order

I would use this sequence:

    Audit the piece-slot semantics and preserve the current champion as a versioned configuration.
    Run the death-rejection ablation, with intervention logging.
    Run the λ10 potential × completion-reward factorial.
    Run the tiny-set overfit diagnostic, without expensive gameplay evaluation.
    If fitting works, train on the existing game-disjoint split with informative-state metrics and an adequate optimization budget.
    Only then collect student-state teacher labels and test closed-loop survival.

Legal seeded fixtures remain worthwhile, and deferring emulator validation until an in-simulator candidate is ready is a reasonable scope decision—as long as claims remain about simulator performance.

Bottom line: §18 is a strong example of evidence improving the project’s direction. §19 is a useful probe, but its negative result is being overinterpreted. It has not shown that imitation lacks headroom, that preview-death rejection is unimportant, or that the current inputs are sufficient. Those are still testable questions—and the cheapest decisive tests are now quite clear.
